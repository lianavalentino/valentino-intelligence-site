# Offer design — Discovery Sprint as the front door

Produced with the `offers` skill (value equation → anatomy audit → one-lever fix). Audience: owner/GM of a 5–75 person business, non-technical. Price points and structure are fixed inputs; everything else is fair game.

Sources: `content/portfolio-content.md`, `business/ops/process/01–07`, one live prospect's prep notes (buyer psychology only — no names here).

## 1. Current offer, stated plainly

| Component | Today |
|---|---|
| Core | "Discovery Sprint": 2 weeks, up to 2 stakeholder interviews, data-readiness scorecard, 3–5 opportunities scored (value × feasibility × data readiness × risk), build plan for top 1–2, slide readout on day 10 |
| Bonuses | None |
| Guarantee | None |
| Scarcity | None |
| Name | "Discovery Sprint" — consultant/dev jargon; an owner can't repeat it to a friend |
| Price | $5,500 fixed, 50% before kickoff, 50% at readout, Net 15 |
| After | $4,500/mo retainer (25 hrs, 30-day notice) or fixed-price SOW (50% deposit); $175/hr anchor |

Delivery promises that already exist but aren't in the offer: approval gate before anything acts, audit log, rollback, handover docs + runbooks + exports, optional ongoing support. These are the strongest anxiety-killers she has and they're currently invisible.

## 2. Value equation — scored for this buyer

| Lever | Score | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | 5 | The buyer wants hours back, nothing falling through cracks, seeing their numbers, and a straight answer to "where do we start with AI." The offer is pitched as a scorecard and an opportunity matrix — deliverables, in consultant language. The outcome of a diagnostic is *certainty about what to do first and what it pays*; that's never named. |
| Perceived likelihood | **4** | Proof exists but is mis-aimed. The ROI calculator → two ~$1M sales is real and strong, but it's a sales tool for a manufacturer. The restaurant publishing system is the most relevant comp (small, non-technical owner, approval gate, running since July 2026) and has no number — fine, but it's not framed as "someone like you." The Nike line cuts both ways: credibility for some owners, "she's enterprise, not for us" for others. No named method. No guarantee. Nothing pre-empts "what if it breaks / who maintains it," which is this buyer's #1 stated fear. |
| Time delay | 7 | Two weeks to a decision is fast and fixed. Two delay surfaces: (a) day 3 waiting on a login (the playbook already warns about this), (b) no first-win moment before day 10. |
| Effort & sacrifice | 6 | Owner effort is actually small (~3 hours over two weeks) but nobody says so. Money risk reads as "$5,500 for a document that leads to a $4,500/mo pitch." Requirement for a "technical point of contact" is a real friction for a 12-person shop with no IT — the fit-call rubric already treats this as a risk. |

**Binding constraint: perceived likelihood (4).** The buyer doesn't yet believe this works for a business their size, or that they'll be able to keep it running after she leaves. Fix this iteration: guarantee + a plain-English name + pre-empting the "breaks / maintains" fear in the offer itself. Proof reframing is a copy job (`copywriting` skill), noted below but not the lever.

Honest lift projection: this is a near-zero-volume offer, so "conversion lift" means fit-call → signed-proposal rate. Adding a guarantee and a repeatable name on a service offer typically moves that noticeably; stacking the bonuses on top is a second, smaller step. Not 5x.

## 3. Anatomy audit and proposals

### 3.1 Core — reframe from deliverables to outcome (no change to what's delivered)

Same two weeks, same playbook. Pitched as: *a ranked list of the 3–5 things worth automating first, each with what it saves, what it costs to build, what it costs to keep running, and how risky it is — written so you can act on it with me or with anyone else.*

Two small delivery additions that cost nothing (the playbook already produces the material):
- **Week-1 note (day 5):** a short written note with the top candidates so far and anything she's found that they should stop doing today. First-win mechanic; the playbook has a draft opportunity list on day 5 anyway.
- **"Cost to keep it running" line per opportunity:** monthly tool cost + expected upkeep hours. Directly answers the maintenance fear inside the sprint deliverable. (Listed as a bonus below because it's the thing to *say out loud*; it's really a core line item.)

State owner effort explicitly: two 45-minute conversations, one hour on day 10, someone who can grant read-only access. About three hours total.

### 3.2 Guarantee

**Idea under evaluation:** "If the roadmap doesn't show at least one opportunity that pays back the sprint fee within 12 months, you don't pay the second half."

Honest read:
- Right shape: conditional, gated at delivery, matches the 50/50 payment split, caps her exposure at $2,750 on work already done. Stress test at 10% invocation: ~$275 per sprint. Fine.
- Wrong trigger. The payback number is *her own estimate*, so she's grading her own homework — a sharp owner sees that immediately. Worse, it puts a thumb on the scale: the guarantee rewards optimistic ROI estimates, and the deliverable's whole value is that the estimates are honest. And in any business with five-plus people, a $5,500/year opportunity always exists, so the condition never fires — which reads hollow, not confident.
- Owners don't dispute an ROI estimate. They dispute "I paid $5,500 and can't use this."

**Chosen guarantee (buyer-judged readout gate, type 4/SLA-ish per guarantee-design.md, same pattern as the fractional-CMO example):**

> If you get to the day-10 review and don't have a plan you'd act on — with me or with anyone else — you don't pay the second half.

Why it's better: one condition, judged by the buyer, tied to the actual purpose of the deliverable (a decision tool), and it kills the "paying for a report" objection head-on. Refund-abuse risk on a B2B diagnostic where the buyer has already paid $2,750, sat two interviews, and granted access is low; the fit call is the filter. Keep the 12-month payback as a *bar she holds herself to* in the deliverable ("every item shows how long until it pays for itself"), not as the trigger.

Build-phase guarantee: don't add a second one. The build already has structural risk reversal — nothing acts without approval, everything is logged and reversible, retainer is 30-day notice, fixed-price builds are milestone-gated. Say that plainly instead of stacking guarantees (guarantee-design.md: multiple stacked guarantees collapse sophistication).

### 3.3 Bonuses (each closes one objection, each costs her little)

| Bonus | Closes | Cost to her |
|---|---|---|
| **"Cost to keep it running" on every item in the plan** — tools per month + expected upkeep hours | "What does this really cost after you leave?" | Zero; it's a line in the scoring matrix |
| **The Owner's Manual** — for every automation built: what it does, how to pause it, how to undo it, who to call. Plain English, one page each. | "Nobody here can maintain this" | Zero marginal; the offboarding checklist already requires runbooks. This names them and promises them up front. |
| **30 days of fix-it support after handover, included on every build** | "What if it breaks after you're gone?" | Bounded hours; would otherwise bill at $175/hr (a real comparable, so it's fine to say) |

Considered and skipped: an "owner's dashboard" (that's a build item, not cheap); a free quarterly check-in call for a year (cheap, but decide first whether it competes with the retainer — it probably feeds it; optional add later). Don't attach dollar values to the first two — no comparable, and the audience reads "worth $X" as a sales trick.

### 3.4 Scarcity — real only

She is one person. A sprint is ten working days of interviews, scoring, and a deck, run alongside retainer clients at 25 hrs/month each. The honest constraint:

> I run one Two-Week Plan at a time. Next open start: **[date]**.

Rules for keeping it honest: the date lives on the site and gets updated when a sprint books; if nothing is booked, it says "now." No spot counts, no timers. Don't publish a cap on build clients unless she will actually enforce one.

### 3.5 Names

Test used: can an owner text a friend "I signed up for *the X*. It's $5,500 and you get [one line]"?

- **Sprint → "The Two-Week Automation Plan."** Outcome- and time-named. "Automation" is more concrete than "AI" for this buyer and doesn't trip hype alarms; "AI" belongs in the subhead. Passes the text test. "Discovery Sprint" stays as the internal/contract name if she wants — the Exhibit A can say "Discovery Sprint (the 'Two-Week Automation Plan')."
- **Build phase → "Build & Handover."** The name carries the promise that answers the biggest fear: you end up owning it. Two ways to pay underneath it: *Monthly* ($4,500/mo, ~25 hours, 30 days' notice) and *Fixed-price project* (quoted in the plan, half up front).
- Readout → "the day-10 review." "Readout" is internal.

### 3.6 Publish the $5,500? **Yes.**

- This audience treats "contact us for pricing" as a signal the number will be scary. A fixed fee *is* the value proposition — predictability against "what will this cost me" — so hiding it hides the point.
- It pre-qualifies. The fit-call rubric marks "shopping for cheapest" as a hard no; a published price filters those before they cost her a free 45 minutes.
- Almost no competitor at this level publishes; it's differentiation for free.
- The guarantee and the "about three hours of your time" line take the sting out.

Also publish: "$4,500/month" for the monthly build option and "fixed-price projects are quoted in your plan." Keep $175/hr, Net 15, and deposit mechanics in the proposal/agreement — the site only needs "half to start, half at the review."

Tradeoff to flag: the Nike credential. Use it once, framed as "ten years building this for big companies, now for businesses that can't afford a mistake." Don't lead with it.

## 4. Site copy

Reading level target: 8th grade or under. No: agents, pipelines, orchestration, LLM, n8n, stack, roadmap, sprint, readout, data-readiness, game-changing, secret, "worth $X," "limited time," "100% guaranteed."

### Headline

**Find out where automation pays off in your business. Two weeks. Fixed price.**

Alt: **Stop doing by hand what a computer should do for you.**

### Subhead

For owners of 5–75 person businesses who know they "should be using AI" and want a straight answer: what to automate first, what it saves, and what it costs to keep running.

### The Two-Week Automation Plan — what you get

- **Two 45-minute conversations.** One with you, one with the person who runs the work day to day. I ask how things actually get done and where time goes.
- **A look at where your information lives.** Spreadsheets, your accounting system, email, your point-of-sale, whatever you use. I rate how ready each one is, so we don't plan around numbers you can't get to.
- **A week-1 note.** By Friday of the first week you get a short note: the top candidates so far, and anything I've found that you should stop doing today.
- **A ranked list of the 3–5 things worth automating first.** For each one: what it saves in hours or dollars, what it costs to build, what it costs to keep running, how long until it pays for itself, and how risky it is. Marked as a quick win or a bigger project.
- **A build plan for the top one or two.** Written so you can act on it with me or hand it to anyone else. No lock-in.
- **A one-hour review on day 10.** I walk you through the plan and you decide in the room, or don't.
- **Your time: about three hours over two weeks.** You'll need someone who can give me read-only access to your systems.

### The guarantee

If you get to the day-10 review and don't have a plan you'd act on — with me or with anyone else — you don't pay the second half.

### Included

- Every item in your plan shows what it costs to keep running: tools per month and upkeep hours. No surprises after I leave.
- Every automation I build comes with an Owner's Manual: what it does, how to pause it, how to undo it, who to call. One page, plain English.
- 30 days of fix-it support after every handover. If something I built breaks in the first month, I fix it. No invoice.

### Price

$5,500, fixed. Half to start, half at the review. No hourly surprises.

I run one plan at a time. Next open start: **[date]**.

### Call to action

**Book a free 30-minute fit call**

No slides. We talk about your business and I tell you honestly whether this is a fit. If it isn't, I'll say so and point you somewhere useful.

### What happens after the plan — Build & Handover

If you want something built, there are two ways to pay: a monthly arrangement at $4,500 a month for about 25 hours of my time, which you can stop with 30 days' notice, or a fixed price for one project, quoted in your plan, half up front. Either way, every automation I build works the same: a person on your team approves before it does anything real, it keeps a record of everything it did, and it can be turned off or undone. When we're done, you get the Owner's Manual, copies of everything, and 30 days of fix-it support. You own it. Ongoing support is there if you want it, not required.

### Trust line (use once, low on the page)

I spent ten years building this kind of thing for large companies. Now I build it for businesses that can't afford a mistake.

### Proof lines (no invented numbers)

- Built a cost-and-savings calculator for a fire-safety equipment maker. It's been used at three industry conferences and helped close two sales of roughly $1 million each.
- Set up a family-run restaurant with no marketing staff so social posts go out only after the owner taps "approve" in Slack. Running since July 2026. Their expense dashboard rebuilds itself every morning from the invoices in their inbox.

## 5. Objection lines

**"What if it breaks?"**
Everything I build has three things: a person approves before it acts, a record of what it did, and an off switch. And you get 30 days of fix-it support after handover. If it breaks in the first month, I fix it and you don't get a bill.

**"We're too small for this."**
If one person spends a few hours a week copying, re-typing, or chasing things down, you're big enough. That's the point of doing the plan first: you find out what it's worth before you spend a dollar on building. I've done this for a family restaurant with no office staff.

**"We already tried Zapier / ChatGPT."**
Good. Then you already know the tools aren't the hard part. The hard part is picking the right job, connecting it to where your information really lives, and making sure it can't quietly do the wrong thing. Sometimes the answer is "keep Zapier, wire it differently." The plan tells you which.
